Flint

Gym wearable

A wearable that estimates how close a lifter is to muscle failure. I designed the circuit board and the housing, then built the app around them.

Year
2025, in progress
Type
Personal project
My role
Electrical, mechanical, and app design
  • PCB design
  • Hand soldering
  • Snap-fit housing
  • 6-axis IMU
  • Phone app
01

Problem

Lifters have no objective way to tell how close a set is to muscle failure, one of the key drivers of strength and muscle growth.

02

Approach

My first idea was EMG on the working muscle. Electrodes were hard to secure and remove, and most muscles are covered by clothing.

I switched to velocity-based training (VBT), which estimates fatigue from how the weight moves. That set three requirements:

  • A low-noise 6-axis IMU to measure acceleration and angular velocity
  • A phone app connection to process and display the data
  • Mounting that lets the IMU track the weight’s movement
03

Electronics

A wearable has to be compact to be comfortable, so a custom PCB was non-negotiable. I designed two revisions and hand-soldered every SMD and through-hole part.

Rev 2 fixed most of rev 1’s problems and needed only a few bodge wires.

Lesson: plan for rotating parts, LEDs, and ports from day one.

04

Housing

Three parts: bottom overmold, top overmold, and dial. I exported the PCB as a 3D model and designed the housing around it.

The PCB and battery slide into the bottom half and the top half locks them in. With 2 mm walls, the snap-fits were the hard part. I ended up with four button-tipped tabs and a center dovetail that guides everything into place.

05

Assembly and app

I assembled the device with a custom velcro strap and built a phone app around it.

The app’s GymScore ring combines several metrics, half of them straight from the device. A device tab updates the firmware from the phone, and a live workout view counts reps and analyzes failure. That view is still in progress. A workout can also be started from the dial on the device.

C.H.I.P

Compact Hazardous-environment Investigation Probe

A smaller, controller-driven rebuild of our hackathon-winning spherical robot, with a clear shell for a future camera.

Year
2025
Type
Personal project
Builds on
H.A.M.S.T.E.R., our hackathon robot
  • Robotics
  • 3D printing
  • Resin finishing
  • ESP32
  • Bluetooth controller
See the hackathon version: H.A.M.S.T.E.R.
01

Goal

Take H.A.M.S.T.E.R., the robot my team built in a 24-hour hackathon, and refine it. I wanted three changes: a smaller body so it deploys more easily, Bluetooth controller support, and a more polished design.

02

Shrink the core

The hackathon robot fastened everything to a laser-cut plate. I replaced it with a custom 3D printed core that packs every component into a smaller footprint.

03

Balance it

Testing showed that the real problem was center of mass. If it does not sit below the point where the wheels touch the shell, the core spins inside the shell and the robot goes nowhere.

I weighed, adjusted, and retested until the design drove reliably.

Mass too high and it just spins in place.

04

Clear shell

I made two shells: one printed in white PETG and one in clear resin. I sanded the resin shell and sprayed it with several finishing coats to get it as transparent as possible, so a future camera can see through it for teleoperation.

05

Controller

I updated the firmware on the ESP32 for wireless control, then wrote code that maps joystick input to the independent rotation of each motor. The result is smooth, responsive driving.

06

Result

A compact, rugged search vehicle with controller support, a clear shell ready for a camera feed, and shells that swap for different jobs.

H.A.M.S.T.E.R.

Hazardous Area Mobility and Search Technology for Emergency Response

A spherical search and rescue robot built in 24 hours at MakeHarvard. Our team took 1st place overall.

Year
2025
Type
Team of 4, 24-hour hackathon
Result
1st place overall, 15+ teams
  • Robotics
  • Rapid prototyping
  • Laser cutting
  • 3D printing
  • Wireless control
See the refined version: C.H.I.P
01

Problem

Natural disasters are becoming more frequent and more intense, and hazardous conditions can delay search operations for days. One of my teammates saw that firsthand during the California wildfires.

We wanted a robust, nimble robot that could search dangerous areas for survivors remotely.

02

Concept

A sphere. The outer shell protects the electronics and rolls over rough terrain, a low center of mass keeps the robot from flipping, and the whole thing is small enough to deploy quickly and drive from a phone.

03

Inner core

Everything mounts to a 12 cm x 12 cm laser-cut base. I made custom holders for the motors, and the wheels point outward near the middle of the body instead of sitting underneath. Inside a sphere, they still reach the shell.

Weights under the base lower the center of mass so the core self-stabilizes.

04

Outer shell

Two 3D printed halves in black PLA. We had no transparent filament, so we added holes instead. As the shell spins, the camera’s frame rate blurs them into a see-through view, like looking past a spinning fan.

05

Result

1st place overall. We also designed and tested a camera and air quality sensors, but ran out of time to integrate them with the drive code.

MakeHarvard’s post about the win

Retro Mechanical Doorbell

Programmable tunes on a real drum

A doorbell that plays programmable tunes on a real drum. Built for a client brief that asked for a retro look, a mechanical sound, and modern features.

Year
2024
Type
Team of 4, course project with a sample client
My role
Sound mechanism concept, drum mounts, inner housing
  • Mechanism design
  • Requirements
  • Solenoid and servo
  • 3D printing
  • Design iteration
01

Client brief

A sample client asked for a retro doorbell with a mechanically actuated sound and modern features. It also had to be pet-friendly and inclusive to use.

We ranked our objectives with a pairwise comparison chart and defined a test for each one:

  • A loud, pleasant mechanical tune, between 60 and 70 dB
  • A retro look, reminiscent of the 1960s
  • Activation that does not depend on most senses or appendages, with sound below 25,000 Hz
  • A customizable tune
02

Concept

The hardest part was a mechanical sound source that could play different pre-programmed songs. My idea was a rain drum, a small drum with several notes.

A solenoid strikes the drum, and a continuous servo rotates it to bring each note under the striker.

One solenoid instead of six.

03

Mount, version 1

My first mount held the drum well. Too well. The tight grip damped the vibration and muted the sound.

04

Mount, version 2

I needed to hold the drum firmly while touching as little of it as possible. The drain hole in the bottom of the drum solved it. A tight-tolerance part grips the drum from the inside and leaves the surface free to ring.

05

Integration

I designed the inner housing that holds every part in place, with a compartment for the electronics under the servo. My teammates tied the solenoid and servo circuit to an infrared sensor, and everything went into a prototype enclosure.

06

Result

The finished doorbell, playing its tune.

Hidden Bike Tracker

An AirTag mount nobody can find

A 3D printed AirTag mount that hides in plain sight on my bike. Designed, printed, and installed in 4 hours.

Year
2024
Type
Personal project
Time
4 hours, start to finish
  • CAD
  • 3D printing
  • Fit testing
01

Problem

I wanted to check my bike’s location from my phone at any time. An AirTag does that, but only if nobody notices it.

02

Design

I had spare black filament, so I picked a black part of the bike and an existing screw to mount to.

The mount installs upside down, so I modeled it inverted. Once it is on the bike, the lid sits on top and the AirTag never rests on it, which keeps the lid from opening and the tag from falling out.

After a lot of measuring and several test prints, it fit.

03

Test

I asked a friend to find it. A few minutes of searching turned up nothing. Even after I flipped over the part of the bike it hides on, the black enclosure blended in and was hard to spot.

E-Bike Trunk

Designed around my 3D printer

A two-door trunk for my e-bike’s rear rack. I chose the manufacturing process first, then designed every part around it.

Year
2024
Type
Personal project
Made with
Bambu A1, PETG and PLA
  • CAD
  • Design for 3D printing
  • Snap-fits
  • Hinges
  • Tolerance testing
01

Problem

I ride an e-bike to get around and was tired of wearing a backpack every time I had to carry something. The bike came with a metal rear rack, so I planned a trunk I could throw things into and close.

02

Plan around the process

I picked the manufacturing process first: my Bambu A1 printer. Then I sketched the concept, measured the rack, and rebuilt it in CAD so I could design the mount against it.

The mount combines a tight diagonal fit with screw holes.

03

Too big to print

The trunk was larger than my print bed. I split the body into two halves that snap-fit together.

04

Lids

My first idea was a single thin lid that opened like a garage door. It turned out to be impractical. I switched to two doors with a hinge on each side, so either half opens on its own.

I test printed hinges at several tolerances before settling on the final fit.

05

Result

Four printed parts. The body halves are white PETG and took about 36 hours each. The lids are black PLA, and one deformed on a hot day.

It has been a convenient, functional addition to my bike.

Next time: PETG lids.

Rod Transport Cart

Calculated, simulated, built, tested

A cart that carries a free-standing metal rod 10 feet and back as fast as possible without tipping it. Calculated by hand, verified in simulation, then built and tested.

Year
2026
Type
Team project, ME 360 at Boston University
Skills
Dynamics, motion studies, motor control
  • Hand calculations
  • SolidWorks Motion
  • Arduino motor control
  • Laser cutting
  • Testing
01

Brief

Move a metal rod standing on top of a cart 10 feet and back, as fast as possible, without knocking it over. That takes two things: a cart, and the fastest velocity profile the rod can survive.

02

Hand calculations

I calculated the maximum acceleration the rod can take before it topples, then mapped that limit to a velocity profile that covers 10 feet.

03

Verify in simulation

I rebuilt the setup in SolidWorks, applied the velocity profile, and measured the reaction force on the rod below, at, and above my calculated limit. The reaction force only reached zero at the calculated value, which confirmed the hand calc.

I also checked the assembly’s degrees of freedom so the study could be trusted.

04

Size the drivetrain

The motor tops out at 240 rpm. I calculated the wheel size needed to follow the velocity profile, programmed it into the rotary motor, and ran the study. The cart reached the maximum acceleration and traveled the full 10 feet.

05

Motor control

The DC motor runs from an Arduino through an amplifier on a 12 V supply.

06

Build

My team and I cut the plywood deck, 3D printed custom axle and motor fixtures, laser cut and sanded the wheels, wired the electronics, and programmed the profile.

07

Test

We ran the cart several times and tuned it between runs.

Rocket Nozzle

Sized from first principles

A bell nozzle designed with BU Rocket Propulsion Group: sized from first principles, modeled in CAD, and checked with FEA.

Year
2024
Type
Team project, BU Rocket Propulsion Group
Skills
Propulsion calculations, CAD, FEA
  • Propulsion calculations
  • CAD
  • FEA
01

Learn

New members of BU Rocket Propulsion Group spend their first semester in a crash course on rocket science that ends with a team nozzle design. I spent two months in a rocketry textbook learning specific impulse, thrust, and the geometry that sets them.

02

Calculate

A teammate and I independently solved for 12 design parameters for a given fuel and set of operating conditions.

03

Model

I modeled the nozzle in CAD from those parameters, using a bell nozzle design.

04

Analyze

A teammate and I ran FEA at our calculated pressures. The first design was too thin in some areas, so I modified it until it was ready to manufacture.

05

Result

The club was not able to manufacture the new-member nozzles for a test fire that semester, because of supply issues and its larger project, Icarus. I still came away with a working understanding of nozzle design from equations to analysis.

SinkXSwim

A pirate ship survival game

A game built in Godot: you are a pirate with the treasure already on board, and the job is keeping the ship afloat until you get home.

Year
2024
Type
Solo project
Engine
Godot
  • Godot
  • Game design
  • Scripting
01

Goal

After my first semester of college, I wanted to sharpen my programming. I had made two games with Pygame and wanted to try a professional engine.

I picked Godot because it is lightweight and its scripting language is close to Python. I worked through a platformer tutorial and added an item of my own, a banana that boosts jump height for a limited time.

02

Game design

I wanted the game to feel chaotic, with the player always running to keep things working. Leaks open below deck and spawn garbage that slows you down. You can only carry so much, so you haul it above deck and throw it overboard.

Each leak also drops wood, the resource for repairs. If the game senses you are struggling, there is a rare chance a leak drops duct tape that seals every leak at once.

03

Build

Over a month I wrote the leak and damage tracking, a progress bar to the destination, animations for the ship breaking and sinking, a title screen, sound effects, and a full tutorial level where a talking parrot shows you how to play.

04

Result

One complete level, published as a demo on itch.io. The project put my programming skills back to work and taught me to apply them to something bigger.

FIRST Robotics

Intake design and a seat at the controls

A season on my high school FIRST Robotics Competition team: I designed the intake, helped build a backup robot, and drove at competition.

Year
2022
Type
Team, LEAP Robotics
My role
Intake design, backup robot, driver
  • Prototyping
  • Iteration
  • Driving
01

Intake, version 1

A row of rubber wheels on a motor-driven axle. The wheels were not rigid enough to fully pull in the game pieces.

02

Intake, version 2

Rubber tubing wrapped around the axle, held by bent metal hanger wire fed through the center. It held extremely well, but the wire made the tube too rigid, the opposite problem.

I shortened the wire and filled the rest with cut zip ties. The center stayed stiff enough to grip the axle and the outer part stayed soft enough to pull the pieces in.

03

Final intake

We combined the wheels and the tubing, and had the intake start upright and drop down to deploy so the robot fit the size limit.

04

Competition

Last-minute fixes meant my co-driver and I could not practice until the day before. I drove the body of the robot while he ran the intake and end effector. After the season, I built a ball launcher for a game at our club’s fair.
